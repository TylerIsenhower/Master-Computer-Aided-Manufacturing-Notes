# Finishing Processes and Grinding

#mmet380 #ch6 #concept

## In Plain English

When standard milling or turning isn't precise enough, we move to finishing processes. **Grinding** is the most common. It uses a wheel made of thousands of tiny abrasive grains (like sandpaper on a wheel). Each grain acts like a tiny cutting tool. Depending on how hard you press, the grain might just rub the surface, plow a groove without removing metal, or actually cut a tiny chip. Because the grains are so small, grinding can produce incredibly smooth surfaces and very tight tolerances.

## Grinding Mechanisms

Abrasive grains interact with the surface in three modes:
1. **Rubbing:** Friction only; no material removal. Generates heat.
2. **Plowing:** Material is pushed to the side, forming a groove but no chip.
3. **Machining:** A tiny chip is formed and removed (actual cutting).

## Abrasive Selection

The type of "grit" matters for the material you are cutting:
- **Aluminum Oxide:** Best for steels and cast irons.
- **Silicon Carbide:** Best for non-ferrous metals (Al, Cu).
- **Cubic Boron Nitride (CBN):** For hardened tool steels and aerospace alloys.
- **Diamond:** For extremely hard materials (ceramics, glass). 
    - *Note:* Diamond cannot be used on steel because the carbon in the diamond reacts with the iron.

## Surface Roughness Parameters

We measure "smoothness" using these parameters:
- **$R_a$ (Average Roughness):** The average height of the peaks and valleys.
- **$R_{rms}$:** Root-mean-square average; similar to $R_a$ but more sensitive to large peaks.
- **$R_t$ (Total Roughness):** Distance from the very highest peak to the very lowest valley.

## Defects in Grinding

Because grinding generates intense local heat, it can cause:
- **Surface Burns:** Discoloration and softening of heat-treated surfaces.
- **Micro-cracks:** Due to thermal shock.
- **Residual Stresses:** Can cause the part to warp later.
