# Cutting Fluids and Wetting

#mmet380 #ch6 #concept

## In Plain English

Machining generates a lot of heat. If the tool gets too hot, it loses its hardness and fails. We use **Cutting Fluids** to cool the tool and part, and to lubricate the area so less heat is generated in the first place. But a fluid is only useful if it can actually "wet" the surface—meaning it spreads out instead of beaded up like water on a freshly waxed car. We measure this using the **Contact Angle**: a small angle means the fluid spreads well (good wetting), while a large angle means it is being repelled (poor wetting).

## Roles of Cutting Fluid

1. **Cooling:** Absorbs heat from the tool and workpiece.
2. **Lubrication:** Reduces friction between the chip and the tool rake face.
3. **Chip Flushing:** Washes away the metal chips so they don't get recut.
4. **Corrosion Protection:** Protects the freshly machined surface from rusting.

## Wetting and Surface Energy

The effectiveness of a fluid depends on its ability to stay in contact with the material.

- **Surface Tension:** The "strength" of the liquid's surface. High surface tension (like water) causes beading.
- **Surface Energy:** The energy of the solid surface.
- **Contact Angle ($\theta$):**
    - $\theta < 90^\circ$: **Partial Wetting** (preferred).
    - $\theta \approx 0^\circ$: **Complete Wetting** (ideal).
    - $\theta > 90^\circ$: **Non-Wetting** (e.g., water on Teflon).

## Cooling Techniques

- **Flood Cooling:** Traditional method; a high-volume stream of fluid covers the work area.
- **Through-the-Tool:** Fluid is pumped through small holes in the center of the drill or tool, delivering it directly to the cutting edge.
- **Minimum Quantity Lubrication (MQL) / Micromist:** A tiny amount of oil (1-5 μm droplets) is mixed with compressed air. It provides lubrication with almost no waste or cleanup but cannot flush heavy chips as well as flood cooling.
